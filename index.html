Part 2. The Bare Infinitive

Theory reproduced from Ашмарина, “The ‘to-infinitive’ and the ‘bare’ infinitive”, pp. 16–17, in full. The teaching point of the whole block is that to is a formal marker, a particle, and not a preposition or part of the stem: the question is never “does this verb take to” but “what licenses the absence of the marker here”.

Terminology to fix at the start

The infinitive without the particle is the bare or zero-marked infinitive; with the particle, the full infinitive or to-infinitive. Students who say “infinitive without to” all lesson have not learnt the term.

The bare infinitive is used

EnvironmentExamples
1. After auxiliary verbs (do, did, will, have, had).I didn’t do that.
2. After modal verbs (except ought to, the modal expressions have (got) to and be to and, in some cases, after the verbs dare and need).We must stay here.
Happiness is not inherited – it’s got to be earned.
How dare you speak to me like that!
3. After verbs of sense perception (verbs of the five senses), for example after hear, see, feel, in a special construction called the complex object.I heard somebody come to the door.
I never saw you look so beautiful before.
I felt somebody touch me on the shoulder.
4. After the verbs to let, make and have in the meaning of a causative arrangement.Did your parents let you stay out late when you were a teenager?
They made me do a lot of useless things.
I had a plumber fix the tap in the bathroom.
I’ll have my secretary type the letter.
5. After the expressions had better, would rather (than), would sooner (than), cannot but, nothing but, cannot choose but.You’d better go home and stay in bed.
I’d rather die than stay here.
She does nothing but talk, talk, talk.
I could not but agree.
6. In sentence patterns beginning with why (not).Why not have lunch right now?
Why pay more if we can pay less?
7. After and, or, except, but, than, rather than, as, like when these conjunctions join two infinitive structures.I’d like to speak and write perfect English.
We had nothing to do except go round the shops.
It is more exciting to go on a trip at the weekend than get stuck at home.
I wanted to become a musician rather than enter a law school.

The five notes, which is where the analysis lives

Note 1 – passivisation restores the marker

If make in the meaning of compelling is used in the passive voice, the infinitive must be preceded by the particle to. The same rule applies to the verbs of sense perception used in the passive voice.

I was made to tell everything to my parents. He was made to sign the paper, though he didn’t want to. He was heard to mention his name. They were seen to go out together.

The explanation to give: when the object of the complex object is promoted to subject position, the infinitival group loses its status as part of a complex object and is re-marked. The marker returns because the structure has changed, not because the verb has.

Note 2 – causative get

When the verb get has the causative meaning, the to-infinitive is used: I can’t get my students to talk. Compare make, which takes the bare form. This pair is worth one minute: same meaning, opposite marking.

Note 3 – to-substitution, that is ellipsis of the infinitive

The particle to is often used without the infinitive if the meaning is clearly understood from the context. However, be and have in the meaning of possession are not dropped.

I couldn’t help you, even if I wanted to. Are you and Sylvia getting married soon? – We hope to. She is more confident now than she used to be. You’ve got more friends than you used to have.

Note 4 – the split infinitive

The particle to can be separated from the infinitive by an adverb. In this case we find the split infinitive. Split infinitives are quite common in English, especially in an informal style.

Though I was put off Josh when I first met him, I began to really like him later. She found a chair and sat down to carefully read the rest of the letter.

This note is the bridge to Part 3, where the construction is treated separately.

Note 5 – free variation

Both the to-infinitive and the bare infinitive can be used after the verb help and in the sentence patterns All I did was …, What I do is …

Please help me (to) clean up the kitchen. All she did was (to) sit and watch. What I do is (to) take the phone calls, send emails, keep track of the incoming and outgoing mail.

How to make the seven environments learnable

Presented as a list of seven they are memorised badly. Group them into three motivated classes and the list halves in size:

  • Grammaticalised verbs – auxiliaries and modals (1, 2). The bare form goes with anything that has ceased to be a full lexical verb.
  • Perception and causation – hear, see, feel; let, make, have (3, 4). The bare form goes with the complex object. Break the structure by passivising and the marker comes back (Note 1).
  • Comparative and co-ordinating frames – had better, would rather … than, nothing but, why not, and the conjunctions of item 7 (5, 6, 7). The bare form appears where a second infinitive is conjoined to a first, or where the frame itself is elliptical.

Part 3. The Split Infinitive

Ашмарина gives the construction one note (Note 4, p. 17). It needs more than that, because students meet it constantly in reading, are told by school teachers that it is wrong, and produce it in writing without knowing what they have produced.

What it is

A split infinitive is an infinitive in which an adverbial modifier stands between the particle to and the verb: to really like, to carefully read, to fully understand. The particle is a separate word, so nothing in the morphology forbids the insertion; the objection to it is a matter of style and of one nineteenth-century prescription, not of grammar.

When it is preferred, and when it is the only clear option
  • When the adverb belongs to the verb and not to the finite predicate. He decided to quickly leave and He decided quickly to leave do not mean the same thing: in the second, quickly can attach to decided. Splitting removes the ambiguity.
  • With adverbs of degree and focus – really, fully, totally, completely, further, even, just, only. I want to fully understand this is normal; I want fully to understand this is stilted and I want to understand this fully shifts the emphasis.
  • When the alternative placement lands the adverb between the verb and its object, which English resists: to thoroughly investigate the mystery, not to investigate thoroughly the mystery.
  • With a negative or restrictive adverb the split is normal: He asked me to never mention it again. Note that this is not the same as the negator: never to mention negates the whole group, to never mention is a split infinitive. Both occur; the first is the neutral form and the one the previous lesson drilled.
When to avoid it
  • In formal academic writing, where a long adverbial phrase inside the infinitive is genuinely clumsy: to, without any further delay, submit the report is unacceptable in a way that to quickly submit is not. The length of the intervening material, not the splitting itself, is what makes it bad.
  • Where the adverb can stand after the whole infinitive group with no loss: to read the letter carefully.

The formulation for the students: the split infinitive is a stylistically marked but fully current construction of present-day English. It is not an error. It is avoided in the most formal registers and it is obligatory whenever the alternative would create ambiguity.

Part 4. The Functions of the Infinitive in the Sentence

Theory from Ашмарина, “Syntactic functions of the infinitive”, pp. 23–28, reproduced with all its illustrative material. This is the part of the topic students find hardest, so Part 5 adds a separate bank of discrimination practice.

First, the unit of analysis

A single infinitive is rarely found in real speech without dependent words. The infinitive together with its dependent words is an infinitive phrase, and it is the phrase as a whole, not the infinitive alone, that fulfils the function.

I decided to talk. – a single infinitive as an object.
I decided to talk to you. – the whole phrase is the object (What did you decide?).
Your idea to talk to the customer is quite reasonable. – the phrase is an attribute to idea (What idea?).

The seven functions

1. Subject

To love and to be loved is the greatest happiness of existence. (Sydney Smith)
To watch him eating really gets on my nerves.

It is more common, however, to use the pattern with the introductory (preparatory) subject it, where the infinitive is the real (notional) subject and follows the predicate:

It is nice to meet you. – What is nice? – To meet you.
It gets on my nerves to watch him eating.

2. Predicative

The infinitive can never be a predicate, but it can be part of one. After the link verb to be it is a predicative, that is, the nominal part of a compound nominal predicate.

His plan was to build a bridge over the river.
My aim is to save £500 for summer holiday.

3. Part of a compound verbal predicate

The compound verbal modal predicate consists of a modal verb, a modal expression or a verb with a modal meaning (have to, be to, be going to, manage, fail, intend, want, wish) plus an infinitive:

You must report it to the police. She is going to write a book. Lily completely failed to see anything sinister behind those words. A priest was always at hand when men and women wished to confess their sins.

The compound verbal aspect predicate consists of a verb like start, begin, continue, cease, used to denoting the beginning, duration, repetition or end of an action, plus an infinitive:

It started to rain. It began to snow. He used to walk to work, but now he doesn’t. If we don’t get a loan, the company will cease to exist.

Note

The infinitive can also be part of a simple predicate, in future tense forms: I’ll talk to him tomorrow. This time next week we’ll be lying on a beach in Rimini. I will have finished the report by Monday.

4. Object

He decided to apply for the job advertised on the website. – What did he decide? – To apply for the job.

After the verbs allow, order, tell, ask, beg, request, teach, instruct we usually find two objects, the first denoting the addressee, the second expressed by an infinitive phrase:

Mum told me to do whatever I liked. The President requested the Defense Minister to conduct large-scale exercises in the borderline regions of the country.

The infinitive used as an object can be preceded by who, what, where, when, why, how, and then the pronoun and the infinitive function together as one object: Excuse me, could you tell me how to get to Victoria Station? I am not sure who to call in case of emergency.

Note

The infinitive phrase can be used as part of a complex object: I’ve never seen you shine so bright. I’ve never seen so many men ask you if you wanted to dance. The construction itself is the subject of a later unit; today it is enough to recognise that the infinitive is not an object on its own here.

5. Adverbial modifier

TypeMarking and examples
of purposeAlways preceded by to; can be introduced by in order and so as (not).
She didn’t want to be seen. She went upstairs to sit in her little back room. She made up an excuse in order to avoid trouble. The government has introduced new laws so as to prevent more road accidents. Move your car to the side of the road so as not to obstruct the traffic.
of resultIn sentence patterns with the adverbs too and enough.
She is clever enough to understand your plan. She is too young to wear such an outfit!
of comparison (manner)Often has an additional meaning of purpose; introduced by as if / as though.

of attendant circumstancesDenotes an action which happened, or did not happen, immediately after the action of the finite verb. Often preceded by never or only.
She woke up to find she was alone in the house. When the war broke out, he joined the army, never to return home again. I opened the window, only to receive an ice cold blast down the whole of my body.

A contrastive node of the whole topic. In Russian the infinitive can modify only abstract nouns. In English its distribution is far wider, and the attributive infinitive often carries an additional meaning of purpose.

Part 5. Identifying the function: a separate bank of practice

Students consistently find this the hardest operation in the topic, and the reason is diagnosable: they try to recognise the function by the look of the sentence instead of running a procedure. The section therefore opens with a procedure, then drills one function at a time, and closes with mixed and minimal-pair work where the same string has different functions.

The procedure, in the order the questions must be asked
  1. Find the finite verb first. Everything else is measured against it. If the infinitive is inside the predicate group (after a modal, after be to / have to / be going to / be able to, after begin, start, continue, cease, used to), stop here: it is part of a compound verbal predicate, modal or aspect. This one step removes about a third of all cases.
  2. Is there a link verb be with the infinitive after it? His plan was to build a bridge. Then it is a predicative. Test: the subject and the infinitive can change places – To build a bridge was his plan.
  3. Is there an introductory it? It is nice to meet you. Then the infinitive is the real subject. Test: replace it with the infinitive phrase and the sentence still stands.
  4. Does the infinitive stand first, before the predicate? Then it is the subject. Test: it answers what together with the predicate.
  5. Is there a noun or an indefinite pronoun immediately before it? Then it is an attribute. Test: ask what kind of or which about that noun, and convert to a relative clause – a chance to watch = a chance which allows you to watch.
  6. Is there too or enough in the sentence? Then it is an adverbial modifier of result. This is the most reliable single signal in the topic.
  7. Can you insert in order before it without changing the sense? Then it is an adverbial modifier of purpose. If in order is impossible and the action simply happened next, it is an adverbial modifier of attendant circumstances; look for only or never, which mark it.
  8. Is it a fixed phrase detached by commas – to tell the truth, to be honest, needless to say? Then it is a parenthesis.
  9. If none of the above, ask the question of the finite verb: What did he decide? What did she promise? If the infinitive answers it, it is an object.
Two warnings about the procedure

Step 5 and step 7 collide constantly. He brought a book to read – attribute to a book, or purpose of brought? The decisive test is not the meaning but the position: if the infinitive can be moved to the front of the sentence, it is an adverbial (To read, he brought a book is odd, so here it is an attribute). Teach the movement test and not the intuition.

Do not let step 1 swallow ordinary objects. He wants to leave is an object, not a compound verbal predicate: want is a full lexical verb with its own meaning of desire. The verbs that make a compound verbal modal predicate are a closed list – modals, modal expressions, and the small group manage, fail, intend, happen, chance, prove, seem, appear. Everything else takes an object.

Part 7. Full syntactic analysis of the sentence

The closing exercise of the lesson. Everything so far has asked for the function of one form; this asks for the whole sentence, with the infinitive in its place among the other parts. It is the only exercise that shows students what the function label is for.

The order of the analysis, to be followed without variation
  1. Type of sentence by structure – simple, compound, complex – and by purpose – declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory.
  2. The subject: what it is expressed by. If there is an introductory it, say so and name the real subject.
  3. The predicate: its type – simple verbal, compound nominal, compound verbal modal, compound verbal aspect – and what each part is expressed by.
  4. The objects: direct, indirect, prepositional; complex object where present.
  5. The attributes: what they modify.
  6. The adverbial modifiers: type of each.
  7. Every infinitive separately: its form – correlation, aspect, voice – and its syntactic function.

Steps 1 to 6 are the ordinary parsing the students already do. Step 7 is today’s addition, and it is done last, so that the function is read off a completed analysis instead of being guessed at first sight.

Worked example

Model analysis. To tell the truth, the letters to be signed were too important to be left on the desk.

  1. A simple sentence, declarative, extended.
  2. Subject: the letters, expressed by a noun in the common case with the definite article.
  3. Predicate: were too important, a compound nominal predicate; the link verb were, the predicative important expressed by an adjective with the intensifier too.
  4. Objects: none.
  5. Attribute: to be signed, modifying the letters.
  6. Adverbial modifiers: to be left on the desk – of result, licensed by too; on the desk – of place, inside it.
  7. The infinitives. To tell – Simple Infinitive Active; function: parenthesis. To be signed – Simple Infinitive Passive; function: attribute to the letters. To be left – Simple Infinitive Passive; function: adverbial modifier of result.
